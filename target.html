Shaping product design for enterprise applications at a national retailer

Designed an enterprise reporting tool for Target's internal digital advertising business, then led design and strategy for StudioConnect, an experimental community app making the move to an enterprise product.

The Situation

I worked on two projects at Target that overlapped in time but had very little else in common. Target's internal digital advertising business needed a reporting tool to replace a fragile Excel file that was hard to maintain. At the same time, StudioConnect, a small internal social app, was moving from an experiment to a formal enterprise product. I started as the designer on the reporting tool. When that project moved into the build phase, Target moved me onto StudioConnect because of the work they saw on the first project.

My Role

On the reporting tool, I was the lead designer. I worked with a development team and with the group building Target's new internal design system, which was separate from their consumer-facing system and made just for internal tools. On StudioConnect, I was part of the core leadership team with the lead developer and product owner. Together we guided the product through its move to enterprise, shipped new features, and standardized on that same internal design system without losing what made the app worth using.

The Work

Marketing reporting tool: replacing a fragile workaround

Target Media Network ran campaign performance reporting out of a custom Excel file. Marketing analysts used it to create infographics, icons, and deck-style PDFs. The file broke often, version control was a constant headache, and every new metric meant rebuilding something that was never meant to be rebuilt.

We wanted to give analysts enough options to tell any story they needed without overwhelming them. Analysts built a report by adding slides from a set of templates in whatever order they wanted, then filling in their numbers, headings, and text. The tool handled the math and the layout for them, so it felt simple to use while still covering the full range of reporting they did.

While we were designing the tool, I heard about the team building Target's new internal design system and component library. I reached out to them directly, and we adopted it for the reporting tool. Our project became one of the first to use the system in practice, and we demoed it as an early proof of concept for what the system could do.

Then Target Media Network rebranded as Roundel, with no advance notice. The new brand had its own fonts, colors, icons, and visual language. We had already finished the deck outputs, so we redesigned all of them from scratch with new layouts, typography, color, and icons. The builder interface held up, but everything the tool produced had to match a brand that didn't exist when the project started.

The tool was fully built and working, but it never rolled out, most likely because internal priorities shifted after the rebrand.

StudioConnect: keeping what made it work

StudioConnect didn't look like anything else Target had built. It was a private, invite-only social network where Target designers talked directly with a curated group of Target guests to get real feedback on upcoming designs and products. It felt a lot like Instagram: personal, community-driven, and more direct than most enterprise feedback channels. That direct line between designers and real guests was rare, and it was worth protecting.

When I joined, the app had just moved from experimental to enterprise. The business owners who built it had stepped back, and a formal team had taken over. My job, along with the lead developer and product owner, was to guide the dev team through that change, ship new features, and standardize on Target's internal design system without turning a community app into just another internal tool.

The hardest part was balancing a lot of competing needs at once. The dev team was stretched thin and needed structure and support. The original business owners had years of vision for the product and cared deeply about where it went. We also needed to keep listening to actual users and make sure every decision kept the sense of community, and of being heard, that made the app work. My role was to hold all of those needs together from a design perspective and find a path forward that didn't give up any of them without a good reason.

Bringing in a formal scrum process helped us with both the process and the people. Retrospectives gave the dev team a set place to be heard. Defined sprints made expectations clear for everyone. I also ran separate meetings with the business owners and Target designers to understand what they needed and test design directions before bringing them to the larger team.

We didn't just adopt the design system. We contributed back to it. StudioConnect needed a color scheme the system didn't have yet, so we created one and shared it with the design system team. That palette let the app and the admin platform feel like one product even though they were built differently.

We shipped user profiles, something the community had never had. We made major improvements to the admin platform, where the team tracked engagement, managed members, and sent rewards. And we worked through a roadmap with far more on it than we could ever build, making the hard calls about what mattered most.

The Outcome

The reporting tool never rolled out, but our early adoption showed what Target's new internal design system could do in practice. StudioConnect launched new features, including user profiles, to a live and active community. The app kept its personality through the transition, which was the goal from the start.

What I Learned

As an outside contractor, I ran into a lot of friction on both projects that came from things I couldn't control: internal politics, competing priorities, and a rebrand nobody saw coming. You can do strong work inside a difficult system and still watch it not ship. What I could control was relationships. I built connections across teams and traded ideas with other people doing interesting UX work at Target. Nobody asked me to, but it seemed important, and those conversations turned out to be some of the most valuable parts of both projects. I learned that as a contractor, the trust I built with people mattered as much as the work I delivered.
