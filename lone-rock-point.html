The Situation

Lone Rock Point is a digital agency focused on public sector web modernization, with clients including NASA, JPL, and the Department of Energy. When I joined, the agency was deliberately growing from embedded contract work into full-service agency work. That meant it needed design and UX capabilities it hadn't needed before, so it hired me as Director of UX Engineering to build that practice.

My Role

There was no template to follow, so I built the practice the same way I'd tell any client to build something: put something together, test it, learn from it, and make it better. Every client engagement has taught me something new, and I've put what I learned back into the practice. Over time my scope has grown to include product ownership of CivicPress, LRP's USWDS-native WordPress theme and the only one of its kind.

The Work

Building a client discovery system

Before I arrived, the agency had no structured way to understand what a client actually needed before building for them. I created the whole discovery sequence from scratch: a detailed project intake survey, a business and user needs workshop, a content creator workshop about internal publishing processes, and a hypothesis workshop to surface assumptions, assess risk, and pair proposed features with real business and user needs. Each workshop has a facilitation script, a Mural board, emails for before and after the workshop, and a summary. At the end of discovery, everything comes together in a findings deck that becomes the foundation for the rest of the project.

I also put all of the agency's client-facing deliverables on a single design system built on the agency's branding. Decks, documentation, and summaries all look like they belong together because I designed them that way.

The practice runs on Lean UX principles. The team works out problem statements, proto-personas, jobs to be done, and hypotheses together with the client instead of handing them over afterward. Clients take part in the research instead of just receiving it. Every workshop ends with a "key takeaways" round where everyone shares what they learned, what they think, or what they're still wondering about. It reliably produces the most honest moments of the session, and clients often say they feel heard.

I built this practice the same way I tell clients to build their products: in small steps, with real feedback at every stage.

AI-assisted research

I've brought AI into LRP's discovery and synthesis work as a collaborator, not a generator. I use it to synthesize workshop findings, pressure-test assumptions, and give clients grounded starting points about their users that they can react to and build on. The goal is always to help clients think more clearly about their own users, not to do that thinking for them.

CivicPress

CivicPress is LRP's USWDS-native WordPress theme. It supports the U.S. Web Design System natively within WordPress's Full Site Editing architecture. For public sector organizations working through OMB M-23-22 and the IDEA Act, a compliant, accessible, and maintainable theme is more than a nice-to-have. It answers a real federal mandate for digital-first public experiences.

I led the first refactor to support Full Site Editing, which is where WordPress theme development was headed. As product owner, I became responsible for leading the CivicPress product sprints, setting the technical and design direction, tracking WordPress core releases to keep the theme compatible, and sending feedback upstream.

Automating docs with AI

I initially created all of the CivicPress documentation by hand: the developer documentation for the theme and the design system documentation the agency customizes for each client. Hand-written documentation goes stale quickly, so I worked with LRP's developers and with AI agents to automate it. The agents used my documentation as an example to build on, and under the team's guidance they wrote their own instructions for how to develop and document CivicPress agentically. Client documentation now lives right in the WordPress admin, with live previews of each component, so editors can see what they're working with while they work. The same approach keeps the developer documentation current as the theme changes.

Figma-to-development workflow

I created the agency's foundational Figma file to match CivicPress, with every theme token set up as a variable, and built out a system of tokens, components, templates, moodboards, layouts, and annotated views. I built it with the development team so it reflects how they actually build, not how a designer imagines they build. The goal was enough structure to work from without a system that turns into its own maintenance burden. The team is still working through the tension between lean iteration and the large deliverables and formal approvals that public sector clients expect.

To close the loop between design and code, I forked an open-source Figma plugin and reworked it to export the Figma variable collections straight into WordPress theme.json format. The plugin reads variable collections from Figma and merges them into the right sections of the JSON file without overwriting existing settings. To make that work, I structured the Figma file very precisely to match what the plugin expects, which also lets the Figma file act as the source of truth for the design system.

The Outcome

The discovery practice is running with real clients, and they respond well to the work. CivicPress is live, and the first client sites on it have already launched. The Figma-to-development workflow is in use and still evolving. What started as a missing capability at a growing agency is now a working practice with documented processes, real tools, and a product attached to it.

What I Learned

When you build a practice from scratch, you don't know what you don't know until you're already doing it. The lean approach helped. Shipping something and improving it always beat waiting until everything felt perfect. Early on, a more deliberate internal discovery phase would have helped me understand the agency's existing client relationships before I designed processes around them. I've gotten better at that with each engagement. It's a good reminder that the principles I apply to client work also apply to building a practice.
